Brussels · Walking + Metro · History + Architecture + Food

A walk through the heart of Brussels.

A deliberately paced route that starts at Brussels-Central, follows the traces of the vanished Putterie quarter, opens onto the Grand-Place and Bourse, then jumps by metro to Montgomery and the Cinquantenaire, through the European Quarter, Avenue Louise, the Royal Palace and Mont des Arts, before returning to the historic centre for Manneken-Pis and a seafood finish. Along the way, keep an eye out for Brussels' own architectural signature: the city that gave the world Art Nouveau, through Victor Horta, before Horta himself helped usher in Art Deco a generation later.

Start: Brussels-Central Walk → Metro → Walk Watch for: Horta · Art Nouveau · Art Deco Finish: seafood & drinks

How to use the tour

Each stage has a direct Google Maps link. Open it when you are ready to move to the next stop. The route is intentionally more than a checklist: the short historical notes are there to make the buildings and streets mean something as you walk through them.

The rhythm

Historic centre → Grand-Place → Bourse → De Brouckère → Metro → European Quarter → Place du Luxembourg → Avenue Louise → Royal Palace → Mont des Arts → Galeries St-Hubert → back to Grand-Place → Manneken-Pis → food.

Take your time around the Grand-Place, the Cinquantenaire and Mont des Arts rather than treating them as quick photo stops.

Architecture thread: Victor Horta bookends this walk — his Art Deco Brussels-Central Station at the start, his Art Nouveau townhouses off Avenue Louise midway, and his own Art Deco Bozar near the end.

1
Start · Horta

Arrive at Brussels-Central

Begin at Brussels-Central Station, right in the middle of the historic city. From here, the Grand-Place, the Royal Quarter and the old centre are all within easy walking distance.

  1. Before you walk off, look up at the station hall itself — it's a Victor Horta design, and it sets up an architectural thread you'll keep meeting on this walk.
  2. Horta drew up the station in 1910–12, late in his career, in the stripped-down, geometric Art Deco manner he moved to after Art Nouveau — the style he himself had pioneered a generation earlier — fell out of fashion and out of budget after the First World War.
  3. Construction dragged on for four decades — halted twice by two world wars and a financial crisis — and Horta died in 1947, five years before it opened. Architect Maxime Brunfaut finished the job in 1952.
  4. That's why the building reads as a hybrid: Horta's Art Deco massing and geometry, finished in the raw concrete, unornamented, function-first language of early-1950s post-war modernism — the mood that would soon harden into Brutalism. Few buildings in Brussels show two architectural eras stitched together so plainly.

2
Walk · The vanished city

Walk down through the Putterie

As you leave Central, walk through the Putterie area. This is one of the most interesting “invisible” pieces of Brussels: the Putterie was once a historic quarter of the city, but much of it was demolished during the construction of the North–South railway connection and Brussels-Central.

The name itself is medieval: it shows up as "Puttenhol" in records from 1280 and 1310, Latinised as "Poteria," and settled into Dutch as "Putterij" and French as "Putterie" — most likely referring to the water wells ("put") that riddled this low, damp slope. That slope runs right down the back of the Cathedral of St. Michael and St. Gudula, whose towers you can pick out rising just above the station; a small Square de la Putterie still marks the spot today.

Fittingly humble company for a cathedral: this was a working-class quarter of narrow lanes and modest houses, the kind of neighbourhood city authorities were only too glad to clear away as "insalubrious" once the railway gave them the excuse. Which tracks with an old anecdotal pattern you'll notice in plenty of European cities — the rough, unglamorous side of town has a funny habit of ending up tucked directly behind the church.

In other words, you are walking through a modern Brussels landscape that sits on top of a neighbourhood that largely disappeared in the 20th century. Look for the surviving fragments and street names that preserve the memory of it.

↗ Walk: Central → Putterie
The present Central Station opened in 1952, and the North–South connection radically reshaped this part of Brussels.
3
Walk · Grand-Place

Enter the Grand-Place

Continue toward the Grand-Place, the great civic and commercial heart of historic Brussels. Its history goes back to a medieval market; the surrounding guild houses and the Gothic Town Hall developed over centuries, while much of the square was rebuilt after the bombardment of 1695.

This is the moment to slow down: walk around the entire square, look up at the guild houses, and take in the contrast between the richly decorated façades and the scale of the space itself.

↗ Walk to the Grand-Place
4
Walk · City & commerce

Continue to the Bourse

Leave the Grand-Place and head toward the Brussels Stock Exchange (Bourse). The monumental building reflects the 19th-century ambition to give Brussels a grand financial and commercial centre. Today, the renovated building is also home to Belgian Beer World.

↗ Walk: Grand-Place → Bourse
5
Walk · De Brouckère

Walk toward the Hôtel Métropole

Continue north toward Place De Brouckère and pause outside the magnificent Hôtel Métropole. Dating from 1895, the building combines eclectic architecture with neo-Renaissance and neo-Rococo influences. Even though the hotel itself closed in 2020, the façade remains one of the great visual landmarks of the square.

1895 is a telling date: it's the very year a young Victor Horta was finishing the Hôtel Tassel across town, usually credited as the first Art Nouveau building anywhere. The Métropole's ornate historicism is a reminder of what Horta was breaking away from.
↗ Walk: Bourse → Hôtel Métropole
🚇 Metro transfer: From De Brouckère, take the metro toward Montgomery. The purpose of this jump is to move from the dense historic centre to one of Brussels' grandest monumental spaces without spending a long time walking through less interesting stretches. Open the route in Google Maps ↗

7
Walk · Cinquantenaire

Walk via Montgomery and Merode to the Cinquantenaire

Make your way through Montgomery and Merode toward the Cinquantenaire. The transition is striking: Brussels gradually opens from its residential streets into the monumental 19th-century park and its enormous triumphal arch.

↗ Walk: Montgomery / Merode → Cinquantenaire
Optional detour: around the park you can look at the monuments and statues connected with Belgium's colonial history in the Congo. This is a useful place to discuss that history rather than simply treating the monuments as decoration.
8
Walk · European Quarter

Continue to Berlaymont and the European Council

From the Cinquantenaire, continue toward the heart of the European Quarter. The Berlaymont is the headquarters of the European Commission, while nearby buildings house the institutions of the European Union, including the European Council.

This part of Brussels gives the tour a completely different character: you have moved from historic Belgium into the political centre of today's European Union.

↗ Walk: Cinquantenaire → Berlaymont / European Council
Optional: wander along Rue de la Loi to experience the institutional heart of the European Quarter.
9
Walk · Place Jourdan

Head to Place Jourdan and Leopold Park

Continue to Place Jourdan and the leafy Leopold Park. This is a good moment to change pace after the monumental European institutions: the square has a lively, local atmosphere and the park provides a green break in the middle of the European Quarter.

And, naturally, this is the moment for one of Brussels' essential food rituals: frites with mayonnaise. Pick up a cone at the traditional fritkot in the square and eat them the Belgian way.

↗ Walk: European Quarter → Place Jourdan / Leopold Park
10
Walk · Hidden Brussels

Climb to the Solvay Steps

Continue toward one of the city's more atmospheric viewpoints: the Solvay Steps. It is a quieter, more intimate part of Brussels and a great place for a photograph.

↗ Walk to the Solvay Steps
Photo stop: this is the place for the classic photo opportunity mentioned in the original itinerary.
11
Walk · European Parliament

Visit the European Parliament

From the Solvay Steps, continue toward the European Parliament. The Parliament's visitors' area includes the Parlamentarium, the European Parliament's free visitors' centre, where you can learn how the institution works and how European legislation is made.

↗ Walk to the European Parliament
If you want to go inside, check the visitor centre's opening arrangements before setting off.
12
Pause · Place du Luxembourg

Have a Belgian beer at Place Lux

Just opposite the Parliament is Place du Luxembourg, a lively square surrounded by cafés, restaurants and the offices of the European Quarter. This is the perfect place for a short Belgian beer break.

At the recommended brewery, try a Vedett or one of the Ginette beers. It is a deliberately relaxed pause before continuing toward the centre.

↗ Open the recommended brewery in Google Maps
13
Walk · Avenue Louise · Art Nouveau

Walk toward Trône and admire Avenue Louise

Leave Place du Luxembourg and head toward Trône, taking in Avenue Louise and its grand urban scale. This is one of Brussels' most prestigious shopping and residential avenues, lined with luxury boutiques, offices and elegant architecture.

This avenue and the streets just off it (Ixelles and Saint-Gilles) are also the heart of Brussels' Art Nouveau district — the style Victor Horta effectively invented here in the 1890s, built on flowing "whiplash" lines, exposed iron and glass, and light-filled interiors.

↗ Walk toward Trône / Avenue Louise
Optional: if you want more shopping, continue along Avenue Louise toward the Apple Store and the surrounding luxury boutiques.
For the architecture fans: Horta's own Hôtel Solvay stands right on this avenue, at 224 Avenue Louise — a private Art Nouveau townhouse he designed down to the doorknobs (1895–1900), now a small museum open only a handful of days a month. His own house nearby in Saint-Gilles is also a museum, if you have time for a longer detour on another day.
14
Walk · Royal Quarter

Continue to the Royal Palace and Royal Park

From Rue de Trône, turn toward the Royal Palace and the Parc de Bruxelles (Royal Park). This brings the route back toward the historic centre and into the monumental heart of the Belgian monarchy.

Take a moment to look at the palace, then cross into the park. The formal layout provides a beautiful contrast with the intimate streets you will encounter later in the old centre.

↗ Walk: Trône → Royal Palace / Royal Park
Pause: the little bar shown in the original itinerary is a good place to sit down, have a drink and rest before continuing into the historic centre.
Bonus: directly across the park from the Royal Palace stands the Palais de la Nation, seat of Belgium's federal parliament. If it happens to be sitting, it's usually possible to visit — worth checking on the day.
15
Walk · Mont des Arts

Climb to Mont des Arts for a panoramic view

Head to Mont des Arts, the terraced garden and cultural complex built on the site of a neighbourhood cleared under Leopold II. Its formal, geometric layout leads up to one of the best panoramic viewpoints in the city: on a clear day you can pick out the Town Hall tower on the Grand-Place and, further off, the Basilica of Koekelberg and even the Atomium.

↗ Walk to Mont des Arts
Bonus: off to one side you can spot the Palais de Justice, often called the largest courthouse in the world at around 26,000 m². It was built in the 19th century under Leopold II, who is said to have seen judicial power as the greatest power a people could hold.
16
Visit · Place Royale

A short stop at the Magritte Museum

Just off Mont des Arts, at Place Royale, is the Magritte Museum, home to the world's largest collection of work by René Magritte — one of the most renowned surrealist artists in the world.

↗ Open the Magritte Museum in Google Maps
17
Walk · Mont des Arts

See the Carillon du Mont des Arts

Look up for the Carillon du Mont des Arts, the sun-shaped clock and bell tower built into the complex near the top of the garden. Its geometric, sunburst design is pure Art Deco — the same style you'll meet again at Brussels-Central and, next stop, at Bozar.

↗ Open the Carillon in Google Maps
The original itinerary notes evocative designs referencing Belgium's colonial-era history in the Congo, including an image of the explorer Henry Morton Stanley — worth a closer look if you're interested, though it's a detail we haven't been able to independently confirm.
18
Walk · Near Central Station · Horta

Pass by Bozar — Horta's other Art Deco landmark

Just to the side, near Brussels-Central Station, is Bozar (the Centre for Fine Arts), the city's landmark venue for modern and contemporary art. Barack Obama gave his March 2014 EU–US relations speech here.

  1. Bozar is a Victor Horta building too — designed in 1923 and opened in 1928, it's the clearest sibling to Brussels-Central: the same architect, working in the same rounded, geometric Art Deco vocabulary he adopted once his earlier Art Nouveau style went out of fashion after the First World War.
  2. The site was brutal to build on: a steep drop between Rue Royale and Rue Ravenstein, a protected sightline from the Royal Palace to the Town Hall spire that Horta couldn't block, and a legal requirement to keep shops at street level. He solved it by burying much of the complex and building in reinforced concrete — an engineering feat as much as a design one.
  3. So between stops 1, 13 and 18 you've now seen Horta's full career arc: Art Nouveau's inventor reinventing himself as one of Brussels' foremost Art Deco architects.
↗ Open Bozar in Google Maps
Local tip: the original itinerary notes this area as a spot where crowds gather to celebrate when Belgium's national football team, the Red Devils, win.
19
Treat · Chocolate

Treat yourself at the Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert

You've earned a break. Duck into the Galerie de la Reine, part of the Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert — Europe's first covered shopping arcade, and still one of the loveliest places in the city just to stand still under the glass roof for a minute.

  1. Walk the length of the arcade and look up at the glass-and-iron roof before you start browsing.
  2. Pop into a couple of the chocolate houses along the way to compare — most will let you taste before you buy.
  3. Stop at Mary, a Belgian Royal Warrant-holding chocolatier founded in 1919 by Mary Delluc, for handmade, artisanal pralines — it's the recommended pick here.
  4. Pick up a small box to share with the team, or just to keep you going for the last stretch.
↗ Walk to the Galerie de la Reine
20
Walk · Old Brussels

Wander back into the historic centre

From the Royal Quarter, let your pace slow down as you make your way back toward the old city — this stretch rewards looking up and around rather than walking it quickly.

  1. Keep an eye out for Masonic influences and symbolism worked into the architecture and street design of the city centre, including a compass motif — a small detail, but a fun one to spot.
  2. As you reach the Grand-Place, look for the Maison du Cygne (House of the Swan), where Karl Marx gathered with fellow exiles and, with Friedrich Engels, drafted the Communist Manifesto in 1847–48.
  3. Nearby, find the Maison du Pigeon (House of the Pigeon) — Victor Hugo's home during his exile in Brussels.
  4. Take a moment on the square itself: after the grand avenues and the European Quarter, this is the payoff view of the whole walk.
↗ Return toward the Grand-Place
Bonus: if you have a few extra minutes, wander the little side streets around the square before moving on — that's where a lot of Brussels' charm hides.
21
Landmark · Old Brussels

Say hello to Manneken-Pis

A short walk from the Grand-Place brings you to Manneken-Pis — Brussels' most famous (and most good-humoured) landmark. Go in with the right expectations and it's a fun couple of minutes rather than a letdown.

  1. Manage expectations: the statue is small, and there's usually a crowd around it — don't build it up too much beforehand.
  2. The bronze figure dates to 1619, made by Jérôme Duquesnoy the Elder, and has come to represent the city's irreverent sense of humour.
  3. If he's dressed for the occasion, that's a genuine Brussels tradition — his wardrobe now runs to nearly 1,000 costumes.
  4. What you're looking at is a replica; the original lives safely at the Brussels City Museum, a short walk away on the Grand-Place.
↗ Visit Manneken-Pis
22
Finale · Fish & drinks

Wind down around the old Fish Market

Finish the day the way Brussels itself grew up: by the water. Head toward Vismet / the old Fish Market, near Sainte-Catherine — the historic port once reached deep into the centre, and although the old harbour basins were filled in long ago, the neighbourhood's seafood identity never left.

Two ways to close it out, depending on the mood:

  1. Classic: settle in at one of the seafood restaurants and terraces around Vismet / Sainte-Catherine for a relaxed, traditional finish.
  2. Modern twist: head instead to Fish Tank, a contemporary seafood bar at 246 Rue Haute in the Marolles, set inside a former fishmonger's shop — a nice way to turn the old fish-trade story into an actual drink-and-food stop.
↗ Old Fish Market / Vismet ↗ Fish Tank · seafood & drinks
Either way, you've covered the historic centre, the European Quarter and the Royal Quarter on foot in one day — take your time over this last stop.
